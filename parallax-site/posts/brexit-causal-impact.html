10 Years on, the True Economic Cost of Brexit Remains Elusive

Over the decade since the vote, UK output per person is estimated to be 5.10% below a synthetic no-Brexit counterfactual. But remove the United States from the comparison group and 98% of the underpeformance goes away. Are we measuring the cost of Brexit, or the inability to keep pace with America?

Parallax Economics·July 2026 13 MIN READ

UK real GDP per capita ran an average of 5.10% below its synthetic no-Brexit counterfactual between mid-2016 and the first quarter of 2026, with a 95% credible interval from 8.29% below to 0.57% above. By the end of the sample, the UK economy is estimated to be around 10% smaller (-9.98% with a 95% CI of −15.39% to +0.92%). Cumulated over the thirty-nine quarters that is about $28,400 of forgone output per person, on an interval running from a shortfall of (95% CI of of -$54,800 to +$8,800). Given this model and data, there is a 93% chance Brexit reduced UK GDP per capita.

This estimate lands inside the ballpark of published literature. However, it is not statistically significant (at 95% CI) and its entire magnitude rests on one comparison country. This piece dives into some of the measurement challenges faced with counterfactual analyses of Brexit to shed light on what we can and cannot measure.

The technique I've used is the one behind almost every Brexit cost number you have read. Take a group of other rich economies, work out the combination of them that best tracked the UK before the referendum, then model that pattern forward. The difference between the actual UK data and that constructed twin is the estimated effect (called a synthetic counterfactual). This weighted blend of other countries' data essentially represents the UK that never voted to leave. We construct ours from sixteen advanced economies and fitted on data from 1999 Q1 to 2016 Q2, with a very tight pre-vote fit (MAPE of 0.55%). The post-treatment analysis take place from 2016 Q3 to 2026 Q1. This invariably overlaps with COVID-related economic disruption which presents some estimation issues. These are noted later in the post, but considered a viable trade-off to ensure structural changes in the UK economy from Brexit - most of which occured from 2021 onwards - are captured.

Brexit has had a visible impact, but it remains statistically insignificant.

The main finding from this research can be seen in the below chart.

There are two things to take from it. First, the a gap between the UK and its twin emerges from 2018 Q1 and has been growing since (even after COVID disruption has passed). This suggests Brexit is having an increasingly negative impact on UK economic performance. Second, the teal line remains within the grey banding. Because the synthetic counterfactual is uncertain, we cannot say with complete confidence that the UK is definitely much worse off. The range of plausible outcomes still includes a scenario where Brexit had little economic impact. This is shown more clearly in the below pointwise and cumulative charts where the uncertainity band does not drop below zero (except in 2 years around COVID).

If we instead use total real GDP instead of GDP per person, the estimated average gap is -5.29%, with a 95% credible interval from -8.42% to -1.61% below the no-Brexit counterfactual. Unlike the GDP per person result, this interval does not include zero, so it can be considered statistically significant. The estimated effects are almost identical in size —5.10% for GDP per person and -5.29% for total GDP. The difference is that the total GDP estimate is more precise, giving a narrower uncertainty interval. Population growth may explain some of this, as the UK's population grew slightly faster than that of the comparison countries, but it is not the whole story. The models also choose different comparison countries, with the total GDP model placing more weight on the United States - more on that later. Which measure matters most depends on the policy relevance: total GDP reflects the size of the economy, while GDP per person reflects living standards. Regardless of model choice, they have the same limitations which are discussed in the following sections.

A single country is driving the difference.

Digging into this finding reveals an interesing pattern. We can drop each comparison country in turn and re-estimate the analysis. If the answer depends on one country, it is not really an answer about the UK.

Thirteen of the sixteen countries barely matter. Drop any one of them and the estimate stays between −5.22% and −4.71%. Drop the United States and it becomes −0.08%, an interval of −1.92% to +2.15%: 98% of the effect gone, a swing of five percentage points. Only two other countries move it by as much as a point, Spain and Canada, and both move it the other way — dropping Spain gives −7.34% and dropping Canada −6.35%, making the gap larger.

That second result is worth pausing on, because the obvious explanation is wrong. Spain grew 12.99% in output per person over the window and Canada 5.22%, against the UK's 6.23%. If the synthetic UK were a simple weighted average, removing a strong performer like Spain should pull the counterfactual down and shrink the gap. It does the opposite. The reason is that this is not an average - the model refits when a country is removed, and what it reaches for is whatever is left that tracked the UK's pre-referendum path. That is overwhelmingly the United States, which grew 20.06%. Spain and Canada both grew more slowly than America, so their presence dilutes it. Take either away and the synthetic UK becomes more American, the counterfactual rises, and the estimated shortfall widens. The pool has one comparator doing the work and fifteen partially offsetting it.

This is not a surprise once you look inside the model. The prior that chooses which countries to use puts almost all its weight on two: Spain, with an 86% chance of being included, and the United States, at 84% and with a much larger coefficient. Canada and Italy pick up scraps with the other twelve being close to irrelevant. Summed across all sixteen, the inclusion probabilities come to 2.32 — the model wants about two countries. The synthetic UK being selected is therfore Spain, United States, and minor rounding.

This design effectively measures UK growth relative to the United States. Whether that diffrence is the cost of Brexit, the effect of an American AI and productivity boom, or both is not something we can conclude from this work.

The same fragility shows up in the literature. Born, Müller, Schularick and Sedláček put a weight of 0.51 on the United States; Springford, J. puts 0.31; Bloom and co-authors' formal synthetic control puts 0.61, and they report openly that dropping the US roughly halves their estimate while dropping any other single country changes almost nothing. On my pool it does not halve the estimate. It erases it.

Beyond reasonable doubt?

The best way to check that the Brexit result is not just a coincidence is to run an in-time placebo test. This means pretending that Brexit happened earlier, when it did not, and seeing whether the model incorrectly finds a large economic effect. The test must be done carefully, using only information that would have been available at that earlier date, so that later events do not influence the results.

To ensure the placebo matches the original analysis, we repeat 39 post-period quarters. Working back from 2016 Q2, this provides a first intervention date of 2006 Q3. A second placebo is conducted with an intervention date of 2004 Q1, reflecting 20 quarters of pre-intervention training data.

On the matched thirty-nine-quarter length, the two fake dates give −2.65% (p = 0.109) and −3.42% (p = 0.099). Neither is significant at the 5% level, and the original estimate of −5.1% on the same basis is larger in absolute size than both. By these checks, the original estimate appears to hold. However, this should be caveated by reflecting the overlapping window of each placebo, alongside the timing around the finacial crisis that may distort findings.

Statistically significant but economically meanginless.

Graham Gudgin and Saite Lu raised an important objection against the CER's version of this work. They showed you could fit UK GDP with an economically meaningless blend of Australia, New Zealand and Greece and recover a Brexit-sized effect. New Zealand and Greece are outside my pool, so I built three arbitrary three-country sets from what I had and ran them on the headline window. Australia, Portugal and Norway give −5.14% (−7.97 to −2.13). Australia, Portugal and Finland give −4.55% (−7.33 to −1.51). Norway, Portugal and Austria give −5.00% (−9.44 to −1.43), with a pre-period fit between 0.80 and 0.96 in every case.

All three land within half a point of the headline, and all three return intervals that exclude zero. In other words, economically meaningless comparison groups produce apparently significant Brexit-sized effects, whilst the full-sample of properly specified comparators does not. This raises deep questions on the validility of such methods and taking headline results at face value.

Less Brexit, more "X-it"?

In this robustness check, we treat each comparison country in turn as though it had been left the EU in 2016 Q3, and see where the UK ranks in the resulting distribution of fake effects.

The UK ranks second most negative of seventeen units, suggesting the negative impact could be real (albeit statistically insignificant). Interestingly, Spain has a larger negative estimate at -5.5%, which is significant. Indeed, the false positive rate is 38% in this sample with 6 countries showing intervals different from zero. A well-behaved design should show almost none.

Pick your bias.

The core challenge with this approach is that there is no perfect comparison group. The countries least affected by Brexit — such as the US, Canada, and Australia — have their own idiosyncratic economic forces, including different fiscal policies and commodity cycles, which may make them less suitable comparisons for the UK.

Meanwhile, the countries most structurally similar to the UK economically are its European trading partners, but they were themselves affected by Brexit. Some may even have benefited from activity relocating away from the UK.

This creates two competing sources of bias: using distant countries may capture differences unrelated to Brexit, while using closer countries may capture the effects of Brexit itself. There is no obvious set of countries that provides a completely clean counterfactual, and there is no reason to assume these two problems cancel each other out.

The consequence is a sign flip. With low-exposure comparators only the estimate is −3.92%. With an EU-heavy pool it is +1.89% — the UK outperforms its counterfactua! With the mixed pool I use for the original estimate (−5.10%), the spread between the two alternative pools is 5.82 percentage points - larger than the estimate itself.

Across twelve reasonable model specifications, the estimated effect of Brexit on GDP per person ranges from −6.72% to +1.89%. In other words, small changes to the model can produce anything from a sizeable negative effect to a small positive one. Only one specification produces a 95% credible interval that excludes zero: −6.72% (−8.63% to −4.79%). However, this is also the model fitted on the shortest pre-Brexit period, making it the least reliable of the twelve.

The biggest source of uncertainty is therefore not the statistical margin of error within any one model, but the choice of model itself. The estimates vary more across the twelve specifications than the uncertainty intervals within each individual specification.

But, what about COVID?

A key limitation is the hetrogenous impact of COVID across the UK and comparator countries. The ONS has documented that the UK's measured 2020 output fall was exaggerated relative to peers, because the UK measured non-market public-sector output — health, education — by direct volume indicators while most comparators used inputs. When schools closed, measured UK education output fell (by 38.8% in Q2 2020) and measured comparator education output did not. That is UK-specific, so no comparison group can net it out, and it biases the estimate negative.

More than half the headline accrues after 2022 when the UK energy shock, the gilt crisis and a UK-specific inflation path were all operating. The comparison group absorbs the common part of those shocks, but it does not absorb the UK-specific part.

Despite these drawbacks, it was chosen for this analysis to include the longest possible time series, which includes COVID and its recovery. This is to ensure that the structural change to the UK's trading arrangements, most of which did not take legal effect until January 2021, are captured.

Speculation remains on the underlying cause.

The obvious next question is what, if anything, is driving the gap. This method cannot tell you, and what follows is a pattern rather than a decomposition. Two things stand out anyway.

First, the timing may be revealing of the underlying drivers. Nothing really happens for six quarters after the vote — the gap is 0.5% or less through 2016 and 2017 — and it only opens from 2018 Q1. If the referendum worked through an immediate revision of growth expectations, which is the mechanism Born and co-authors identify, the divergence should start when the expectations changed. On the other hand, one could argue the trading relationship did not change immediately (not until 2021), limiting any immediate impacts.

Second, as reported by Bloom et. al, UK gross fixed capital formation has stagnated in recent years. Against a counterfactual this is down -3.27%, albeit insignficant with a 95% credible interval of -7.4% to +0.4%. Whether this is a Brexit artefact or stalled post-COVID recovery remains open for debate.

Verdict? Probably bad, but precisely unknown.

Every headline Brexit figure you have seen comes from this design. Born, Müller, Schularick and Sedláček report a gap of -2.4% by end-2018 with a robustness range of -1.7% to -2.5%. Springford and the Centre for European Reform report -5.5% by 2022 Q2. Bloom and co-authors, the most careful of them, report -6% to -8% by end-2025 and — to their credit — publish five weighting schemes spanning 4% to 11% precisely because the answer is unstable alongside supporting micro methods. Alabrese and co-authors reach 5 to 10 points using sub-national controls. My −5.10% sits in the middle of that crowd. To be clear, I'm not saying these other findings are wrong, just a word of caution around false precision.

Overeliance on a single country, abitrary comparison groups, and statistical insignificance a problematic. But the main issue is that these models cannot say why the divergence occurred. Any shock that hit the UK asymmetrically after 2016 — American fiscal policy, differential pandemic measurement, energy exposure — is loaded silently onto the Brexit coefficient. No amount of pre-period fit fixes that.

So, where does all this leave us? An average 5% shortfall in output per person over 10 years is a compelling result and consistent with published literature. We know from business surveys and anecodtal evidence, Brexit has increased trade frictions with the UK's nearest partners - something that is not conducive to strong angrowth outlook.

However, a 98% dependence on one comparator, and three meaningless three-country blends that reproduce the number with tighter intervals should raise caution with precise estimates. The aggregate approach has produced a decade of numbers that agree with each other but struggle to defend themselves. We must do more to better understand the consequences of Brexit on the UK economy.

Maybe all we need is another 10 years of data to try again. This time will be different...

Outcome: real GDP per capita, USD PPP at constant 2020 prices, seasonally adjusted and annualised, from the OECD Quarterly National Accounts, single extraction 2026-07-26 (sdmx.oecd.org). Estimation is a Bayesian structural time-series model — local level plus spike-and-slab regression on the comparison panel — fitted by MCMC on the pre-period only, with the counterfactual taken as the posterior predictive distribution: 10,000 iterations for named specifications, 5,000 for placebos and leave-one-out runs, level prior 0.01, seasonality off because the series are already adjusted, alpha 0.05, master seed 20160623 with a distinct derived seed per fit, about 130 fits in total. Japan is excluded because the OECD publishes quarterly per-capita GDP for it only from 2007 Q3; Ireland is excluded from the headline pool because multinational profit-shifting makes its national accounts an unreliable comparator, and is reported as a sensitivity that changes nothing because the model never selects it.
